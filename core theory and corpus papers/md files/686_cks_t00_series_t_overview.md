## Abstract

Series T is the trilogy-wide concept disambiguation series in the CKS derivation note corpus. Where Series CC established the inheritance structure across the three CKS papers — showing that Paper 3's commitments build on and extend Paper 1's and Paper 2's — Series T addresses a different prior-art requirement: establishing that the same architectural terms used across multiple papers carry scope-specific meanings that are nonetheless expressions of the same underlying primitive. This note is the Series T overview. It states the purpose and prior-art function of disambiguation notes, identifies the source document (the trilogy ambiguity map) that catalogs the specific concepts requiring disambiguation, describes the five T subseries and the concept categories they cover, and establishes the consistent note structure applied to every Series T note. Series T comprises approximately fifty notes spanning five subseries (T1–T5) plus a closing note, covering core governance concepts, evolution concepts, architectural concepts, operational concepts, and population-and-scale concepts.

## 1. Purpose of Series T

The CKS trilogy reuses architectural terms across its three papers. "Substrate," "cell," "authority," "conflict," "mating," "perimeter," and related terms appear in Paper 1, Paper 2, Paper 3, or some combination of all three. In each case, the term refers to the same underlying architectural primitive — but at a different coordination scope. Substrate in Paper 1 is the cell-scope coordination medium. Substrate in Paper 2 includes the DNA, action, and harness layers within a Self. Substrate in Paper 3 is the shared medium that spans multiple Selves' home perimeters for the duration of an inter-Self coordination event. The same object, the same six founding commitments, the same human-governed authority architecture — operating at progressively larger scopes.

This cross-paper reuse is architecturally intentional. The substrate-mediator ladder framing in Paper 3 articulates precisely this structure: the same human-governed substrate-mediator commitment extends to each successive rung of the coordination hierarchy without changing its foundational architectural obligations. What changes at each rung is the perimeter the substrate spans, not the nature of the substrate or the authority it is governed under.

Reuse of this kind creates a specific prior-art exposure. An adversary examining the trilogy could claim that "substrate" in Paper 1 and "shared substrate" in Paper 3 are different inventions because they operate at different scopes and under different governance configurations. The same argument could be applied to cell, aspect, Self, mating, conflict handling, or any other concept whose scope expands from paper to paper. If accepted, this argument would allow a later patent applicant to claim novelty for one rung of the architecture while the earlier rungs exist as prior art — a scope-splitting strategy that the disambiguation notes are designed to foreclose.

Series T establishes, for each such concept, a precise statement of its scope-specific meaning in each paper and the architectural commitment that unifies those meanings. Every Series T note deposits prior art demonstrating that the concept is one primitive at progressively larger coordination scopes, not a family of distinct inventions that happen to share a name.

## 2. Disambiguation Is Not Inheritance

Series T addresses a different analytical task than Series CC, and the distinction matters for understanding what the notes establish.

Series CC — the Paper 3 cross-derivation series — established the inheritance structure of the trilogy: which Paper 3 commitments directly inherit from Paper 1 commitments, which inherit from Paper 2 commitments, and at what points Paper 3 extends or augments the inherited structure. An inheritance note documents the dependency relationship: Paper 3's Claim X is grounded in Paper 1's Commitment Y. Inheritance notes answer the question "where does this Paper 3 commitment come from?"

Series T disambiguation notes answer a different question: "what does this term mean in this paper, and is it the same thing as what it means in that paper?" A disambiguation note does not assert a dependency relationship. It asserts an identity relationship — specifically, the identity of an architectural primitive across different coordination scopes. A disambiguation note for "conflict" does not claim that Paper 3's three-tier inter-Self conflict handling depends on Paper 1's conflict-as-first-class commitment (that relationship is established in Series CC). It claims that what Paper 1 calls "conflict" and what Paper 3 calls "conflict" name the same architectural object — a governance event involving competing substrate-level states — at cell scope and inter-Self scope respectively.

This distinction has practical consequences for prior-art coverage. Inheritance coverage blocks the argument "Paper 3's inter-Self conflict handling is new because Paper 1 only covers cell-level conflicts." Disambiguation coverage blocks the argument "Paper 3's use of 'conflict' is a different concept from Paper 1's use of 'conflict,' so they cover different inventive territory." Both arguments need to be foreclosed; Series CC and Series T address them respectively.

## 3. The Ambiguity Map as Source Document

The concepts requiring disambiguation in Series T were cataloged in the working reference document `cks_trilogy_ambiguity_map.md`, prepared as part of the pre-Series-D preparation work for the derivation note corpus. The ambiguity map identifies every term that appears in multiple trilogy papers with scope-specific meanings, documents each paper's usage, states the unifying architectural claim, identifies the adversary exploitation risk, and specifies what prior-art coverage of that entry forecloses.

The ambiguity map is the authoritative source for Series T. Every concept covered in T1 through T5 has a corresponding entry in the map. The map was constructed by reading across all three papers and identifying terms that satisfy two conditions: (a) the term appears in at least two papers, and (b) the term's meaning shifts in scope, application, or governance configuration from one paper to the next in a way that an adversary could exploit to argue discontinuity.

Fifteen concepts were identified in the map's initial pass, covering substrate, cell, aspect, Self, mating versus full-aspect integration (FAI), conflict handling, evolution mechanisms, governance perimeter, authority, provenance, lineage, content-domain, DNA and action layers, expression, and governance authority distribution. Series T distributes these concepts across five subseries organized by category.

## 4. Five T Subseries

Series T organizes its approximately fifty notes into five subseries, each covering a category of concepts with related disambiguation requirements. The five categories are not mutually exclusive — substrate, for instance, has governance dimensions (T1) and architectural dimensions (T3) — but the categorical organization ensures that readers seeking disambiguation notes for a specific concept category can locate them efficiently.

**T1 — Core Governance Concept Disambiguation (~10 notes, #T1.01–T1.10).**
T1 covers concepts that sit at the center of the governance architecture across all three papers: conflict, authority, governance record, and substrate as the governed coordination medium. These concepts appear in Paper 1 as the foundational commitments of cell-scope governance and are extended in Papers 2 and 3 to Self scope and inter-Self scope respectively. T1 notes establish that governance concepts are scope-extended, not scope-replaced: the authority architecture of Paper 1 is not superseded by Paper 2 or Paper 3 but instantiated at larger coordination perimeters.

**T2 — Evolution Concept Disambiguation (~10 notes, #T2.01–T2.10).**
T2 covers concepts from the evolution architecture: directed selection, mutation, action-feedback loops, and mating as an intra-Self merge primitive versus full-aspect integration (FAI) as an inter-Self merge primitive. These concepts originate in Paper 2 and are extended in Paper 3. T2 notes establish the scope-specific distinctions — mating produces governed offspring entities within a Self; FAI produces evolution outputs that each participating Self ingests into its home substrate — while establishing the shared architectural commitment that both operations are instances of the same governed merge primitive at different coordination scopes.

**T3 — Architectural Concept Disambiguation (~10 notes, #T3.01–T3.10).**
T3 covers the structural objects of the coordination architecture: cell, aspect, Self, perimeter, and substrate in its architectural rather than governance-authority dimension. Cell appears in Paper 1 as the atomic coordination unit and in Papers 2 and 3 in increasingly nested coordination contexts. Aspect appears in Paper 2 as the within-Self coordination unit and in Paper 3 as the unit of exchange in FAI. Self appears in Paper 2 as the integration architecture and in Paper 3 as the unit of FAI participation. T3 notes establish that each structural object retains its defining architectural identity — its composition rules, its lifecycle, its substrate relationship — as the coordination scope expands.

**T4 — Operational Concept Disambiguation (~10 notes, #T4.01–T4.10).**
T4 covers operational concepts: configuration and orchestration rules, record and substrate content, accountability, provenance, lineage, and inspection. These concepts describe how the architecture functions and how its governance is exercised at the operational level. They appear across all three papers with consistent meaning but different implementation specifics — provenance in Paper 1 names six metadata fields; in Paper 2 it encompasses full entity-lifecycle lineage chains; in Paper 3 it extends to cross-perimeter provenance traveling with contributed aspect content. T4 notes establish that operational concepts share the same accountability structure across papers even as their scope-specific implementations differ.

**T5 — Population and Scale Concept Disambiguation (~8 notes, #T5.01–T5.08).**
T5 covers concepts that appear primarily in Paper 3, particularly in Paper 3's Claim 6, at scales not present in Papers 1 and 2: network topology, population dynamics, collective evolution, and the governance commitments that apply when many Selves coordinate at population scale. T5 notes establish that population-scale concepts are extensions of the substrate-mediator commitment to its largest coordination scope (rung 6 of the substrate-mediator ladder) and do not introduce a new architectural object. They also establish the scope boundary: population-scale commitments apply at the population rung and do not retroactively alter the commitments that govern cell, Self, or inter-Self coordination.

## 5. Consistent Note Structure for All T Notes

Every Series T note follows a consistent structure, regardless of which subseries it belongs to:

1. **Concept identification.** The term being disambiguated, stated precisely.
2. **Paper 1 usage.** The scope-specific meaning of the concept in Paper 1, or an explicit statement that the concept does not appear in Paper 1.
3. **Paper 2 usage.** The scope-specific meaning in Paper 2, or a statement of absence.
4. **Paper 3 usage.** The scope-specific meaning in Paper 3.
5. **The disambiguation.** A precise statement of how the scope-specific usages relate to each other: the architectural commitment that unifies them, the scope differences that distinguish them, and what each scope-specific usage covers and does not cover.
6. **Prior-art disambiguation claim.** A statement of what the note's prior-art deposit forecloses — specifically, which adversarial ambiguity arguments the note eliminates.

This structure ensures that every T note is self-contained as a prior-art document and can be read without reference to other T notes. A reader who encounters "substrate" in Paper 3 and needs to know how it relates to "substrate" in Paper 1 can read the single T1 substrate note and obtain a complete statement of the relationship without reconstructing it from first principles.

## 6. Series T Scope and Prior-Art Coverage

Series T comprises approximately fifty notes (#686–#735), covering the five subseries T1 through T5, preceded by this overview note (T.00) and followed by a Series T closing note (T.CLOSE) that provides a complete cross-reference index of all disambiguated concepts.

The series is designed to work in conjunction with, not in place of, Series CC. A complete prior-art position on any given cross-paper concept requires both an inheritance note (establishing the genealogy of commitments) and a disambiguation note (establishing the scope-specific precision of the shared term). Readers working through the derivation note corpus should treat Series CC and Series T as complementary layers of the same prior-art position.

Every concept in the ambiguity map receives at least one disambiguation note. Concepts that appear in multiple subseries categories — substrate is the clearest case, appearing in both T1 (governance dimension) and T3 (architectural-object dimension) — receive notes in each relevant subseries, with explicit cross-references between them so that the complete disambiguation picture for each concept can be assembled from the relevant notes.

The prior-art function of the complete Series T corpus is to establish, for every architectural term that an adversary might characterize as ambiguous or scope-inconsistent across the CKS trilogy, a precisely dated and deposited prior-art record demonstrating the opposite: that the term is used with consistent architectural meaning across the trilogy, with scope-specific applications that are deliberate extensions of the same underlying primitive rather than distinct inventions.
